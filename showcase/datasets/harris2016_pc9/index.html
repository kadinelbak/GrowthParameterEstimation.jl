PC9 parental and sublines, untreated

➖ Not directly comparable with the paper

The paper found: Introduced the drug-induced proliferation (DIP) rate: after a delay, treated populations grow or shrink exponentially, and the DIP rate is a better drug-response metric than an end-point count.

This page found: Untreated wells only: plain exponential growth is the pick on 10 of 13 wells, consistent with the paper's assumption; the pooled pick is power law.

Why they differ / caveats: The paper's results are about erlotinib-treated wells, which this page does not fit. Only the exponential-growth assumption for untreated cells can be checked, and it holds per well.

Harris LA et al. (2016) Nat Methods 13:497-500 · doi:10.1038/nmeth.3852 · source: https://github.com/QuLab-VU/DIPrateNatMeth2016 · licence: none stated (repository)

PC9 lung cancer cells (EGFR-mutant) and three single-cell-derived sublines (DS3, DS4, DS5), counted by automated imaging every few hours. Harris and colleagues used these plates to introduce the drug-induced proliferation (DIP) rate: after a short delay, treated populations settle into steady exponential growth or decline, and the slope of log counts is a better drug-response metric than an end-point count.

What this page fits. Only the untreated wells (parental growth curves and the DMSO controls of the dose plates, 13 curves with enough points). The erlotinib wells need a drug-response term and are set aside for a later treated-data page.

Reading the result. Per well the rule picks plain exponential growth on 10 of 13 curves, which is what the DIP idea assumes for untreated cells over this window. The dataset-level pick is power law only because the summed BIC is dominated by a few wells that slow near confluence; the per-curve counts are the better summary here.

Package diagnostics

The package's built-in tools, run on every model in the panel on the same 12 evenly spaced curves, each starting from its showcase fit: fisher_information, profile_likelihood, bootstrap_joint_fit (residual, 40 resamples) and synthetic_recovery_benchmark (20 simulated data sets per curve), plus 5-fold cross-validation. Script: showcase/analyses/package_checks.jl.

The 10% proportional error sets the weights; its overall size is estimated from each fit's residuals (the same maximum-likelihood scale the BIC and the showcase profile use). Here the residual scatter is a median 1.5× the nominal 10%, so every interval and simulated data set is calibrated to the scatter the data really have.

❌ Fisher intervals are not reliable for the pick

For power law, the Fisher (Wald) interval is within a factor of 1.5 of the profile interval for 6 of 24 parameter-curve pairs. Where they disagree, trust the profile and bootstrap intervals: the Fisher interval assumes the likelihood is quadratic around the estimate.

Profile interval closes: the 95% interval ends inside the parameter bounds on both sides. Cross-validation: the first point is the initial condition and stays in every training set; the others are dealt into 5 interleaved folds, each refitted with the showcase objective and predicted from the full curve, so this tests interpolation. A fold counts as converged when its fit reaches a weighted error per point within 4× the full-curve fit's (retried with up to 32 starts); folds that never do are not scored. The forecast check above tests extrapolation.

The package profile uses the package's default grid plus points at ±0.5 to ±4 Fisher standard errors around the estimate. profile_likelihood reports the outermost grid points inside the cutoff, which reads about 20% narrow at this spacing, so the widths here interpolate the crossing between grid points. The showcase profile searches outward and bisects the crossing, and uses the exact log-likelihood ratio n·log(wSSE/wSSE₀) rather than its quadratic form, so it reads slightly wider.
